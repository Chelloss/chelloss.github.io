Surgeons Cut the Brain's Bridge to Stop Seizures. Does That Create Two People?

To stop severe epilepsy, surgeons sometimes cut the corpus callosum, the main bridge between the brain's two halves, so a seizure can't spread. In the 1960s and '70s Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga tested these patients: things seen on the left go to the right hemisphere, while speech is mostly run by the left, so one patient said he saw nothing yet his left hand drew the picture. Their data suggested that splitting the brain might split the person, and Sperry won the 1981 Nobel Prize. In 2017, a team testing two split-brain patients found they could respond to things anywhere in view with either hand or by speaking: the team argued that perception was split but the person was not. It was only two patients, so the debate continues. Brain glows are illustrative. Educational content, not medical advice.
