In 1920, Scientists Taught a Baby to Fear a White Rat. What Happened to Him?

In 1920 John Watson and Rosalie Rayner at Johns Hopkins conditioned an 11-month-old boy, 'Little Albert', to fear a white rat by striking a steel bar behind him whenever he touched it; the fear seemed to spread to other furry things, and his mother withdrew him before any attempt to undo it. In 2009 Hall Beck's team proposed he was Douglas Merritte, who died aged six of hydrocephalus. In 2014 Russell Powell's team proposed another candidate, Albert Barger, whose recorded weight closely matched Little Albert's; if they are right, he lived to 87 and his niece recalled a mild dislike of dogs, though nobody can tie that to the experiment. The identity is still disputed. The baby is shown only as a faceless clay reconstruction; no distress is shown. Educational content, not medical advice.

The story

They taught a baby to fear. On purpose.1,4 In 1920, John Watson and Rosalie Rayner showed an eleven-month-old a white rat.1,4 Each time he touched it, they struck a steel bar behind his back.1,4

Soon the rat alone seemed to frighten him, and other furry things too.1,2 His mother took him away. For decades, nobody knew who he was.1,4 In 2009, one team proposed Douglas Merritte, who died of an illness at six.1,2,4

In 2014, a second team proposed another nurse's baby, recorded as Albert.2,4 His recorded weight closely matched Little Albert's.1,4 If they're right, he lived to eighty-seven. His niece recalled a mild dislike of dogs, which nobody can tie to the experiment. The teams still disagree.1,3,4
