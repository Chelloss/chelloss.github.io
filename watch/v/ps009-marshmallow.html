The Marshmallow Test Seemed to Predict Kids' Futures. A 2018 Study of 900+ Children Found Less

In Walter Mischel's marshmallow test, preschoolers could eat one treat now or wait for two, and follow-ups linked longer waiting to better outcomes as teenagers. In 2018, Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan used data on more than 900 children from a more diverse US study, focusing on children whose mothers had not finished college: waiting longer still predicted slightly better achievement at 15, but the link was half the size of the original, and it shrank by about two thirds once family background, early ability and the home environment were taken into account. Mischel's team had long said larger samples might show smaller effects. Educational content, not medical advice.

The story

One treat now. Or two later.1,3 Psychologist Walter Mischel left preschoolers alone with a marshmallow.1,3 Hold out, and you'd get a second one.1,3

Years later, kids who waited longer were doing better as teens.1,2 So willpower at four seemed to predict the future.2,4 But the original children were a small, narrow group.1,3

In 2018, researchers used data on over nine hundred US children, focusing on those whose mothers hadn't finished college.2,3,4 Waiters still did a bit better in school at fifteen. But the link was half as strong.1,2 Then the researchers adjusted the numbers.2,3

The link shrank by two thirds once family background and home life were counted.1,2,3 Waiting for a marshmallow, it turns out, is partly tangled up with the life around you.1,3
